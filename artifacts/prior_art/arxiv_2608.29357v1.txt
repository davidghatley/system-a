Abstract

    

Multimodal search agents answer visual questions by interleaving image understanding, web retrieval, tool use, and evidence synthesis. Strong systems exist, but in two expensive regimes: proprietary frontier models such as GPT-5 and Gemini, or large open vision-language backbones trained with substantial agentic data and reinforcement learning. We ask a different question: when released agent trajectories are distilled into much smaller backbones under a single-node budget, what is actually transferred? We study this with LiteSearch-VL, a low-compute recipe for Qwen3-VL-2B and Qwen3-VL-4B that uses only released OpenSearch-VL trajectories, parameter-efficient LoRA adapters, and synthetic step-level preferences: DPO on GPT-5-generated hard negatives targeting five local failure modes (
premature answer, wrong tool, weak query, repeated query, ignored image
). Across 12,400 GPT-5-judged rollouts on SimpleVQA, FVQA, LiveVQA, and VDR-Bench-testmini, the dominant effect is behavioral rather than a uniform accuracy lift: full-trajectory supervised fine-tuning transfers the 
agent contract
, taking the 2B model from almost never emitting a usable answer (1,237/1,240 
no_answer
 rollouts) to 28.4% macro Pass@1, matching or slightly exceeding the off-the-shelf 4B base (25.6%). Synthetic preference learning and compact tool distillation act as refinements rather than phase transitions (best 4B configuration: 30.8% macro Pass@1). Finally, a controlled VDR step-budget ablation shows that extra search turns convert abstentions into 
wrong_entity
 errors rather than correct answers, identifying answer verification, not search depth, as the next bottleneck for small multimodal agents.

  

1 
Introduction

Knowledge-intensive visual questions often cannot be answered from the image alone: a model may need to identify an entity in the scene, search for external evidence, inspect a crop or an OCR region, and decide when the accumulated evidence is sufficient to commit to a final answer. This has motivated multimodal search agents that interleave reasoning with retrieval and image-processing tools rather than producing a single direct response 
[
1
, 
2
, 
3
]
. The most capable systems, however, are expensive to reproduce: they either depend on proprietary frontier models such as GPT-5 
[
4
]
 and Gemini 
[
5
]
, or train large open VLM backbones with long-horizon agentic reinforcement learning.

OpenSearch-VL 
[
3
]
 changed the data landscape by releasing a 36K corpus of tool-interleaved teacher trajectories, an 8K RL prompt set, and a detailed seven-tool environment, but its strongest models are trained in a substantially larger regime than many research groups can access (agentic SFT on 256 H20 GPUs, then multi-turn GRPO on 64 H20 GPUs). This raises a separate question from frontier performance: 
what exactly do released multimodal search trajectories teach small VLMs?
 Do they transfer factual search competence, or do they first teach the more basic agent contract—how to format tool calls, use image handles, integrate observations, and stop searching with a final answer?

We study this question in a deliberately constrained setting: starting from Qwen3-VL-2B and Qwen3-VL-4B, we train only LoRA adapters (vision tower frozen, multimodal projector trainable) on the released SearchVL-SFT-36k trajectories, construct a compact three-tool distilled variant to probe tool-surface complexity, and build synthetic step-level DPO pairs by asking GPT-5 to generate plausible local mistakes at teacher decision points.

Our evaluation covers ten model cells across four short-answer, knowledge-intensive benchmarks: SimpleVQA, FVQA, LiveVQA, and VDR-Bench-testmini. The clearest result is that SFT transfers the 
agent contract
: the 2B base model emits no usable final answer on 1,237 of 1,240 questions (0.1% macro Pass@1), while after one epoch of full-trajectory SFT the same backbone reaches 28.4% macro Pass@1 and matches the 4B base on the three easier benchmarks. This is not simply a scaling result: the 4B base already follows the contract on many questions, so its SFT gain is much smaller.

The later stages are more diagnostic than transformative: synthetic step-level DPO improves the macro average by +1.0 points on 2B and +1.7 points on 4B, with the largest localized gain on FVQA but a non-uniform effect across domains; the compact three-tool interface reduces performance at 2B, is nearly cost-free at 4B on average, and strongest on FVQA; and increasing the VDR step budget from 5 to 8 reduces 
no_answer
 substantially but barely changes Pass@1—the recovered rollouts mostly become 
wrong_entity
, pointing at verification, not search depth, as the remaining bottleneck.

A low-compute recipe for 2B/4B multimodal search agents from released trajectories on one eight-GPU node.

(i) SFT transfers the agent contract, especially at 2B.

The 2B base model fails primarily as an agent: it does not emit a usable

(ii) Synthetic step-level DPO is a refinement, not the phase transition.

On top of full-trajectory SFT, the synthetic DPO pairs add +1.0 average points on 2B and +1.7 average points on 4B. The largest localized delta is on FVQA at 4B (61.0

(iii) Step-level SFT changes the terminal-behavior trade-off.

(iv) A compact tool interface is not automatically easier.

Discussion and Conclusion

Where SFT helps and where it doesn’t.

The 2B base model is essentially non-functional as a search agent, emitting a final 
&lt;response&gt;
 on only 3 of 1,240 questions (one of them correct); SFT repairs this, but the resulting agent still produces 
wrong_entity
 on roughly 30% of questions, so entity grounding and evidence checking remain unsolved. The 4B base already follows the contract on many questions; SFT mostly polishes its tool selection.

Assessment of synthetic DPO.

The evidence supports a qualified version of the targeting claim: average Pass@1 grows on both backbones, with the cleanest single signal on 4B FVQA (+8.5 points) alongside a 13% reduction in 
no_answer
 over all four benchmarks (587 

→

\to

 511, Table 

6

). Conversely, LiveVQA at 4B loses 2.8 points after DPO, and the 2B picture is closer to a redistribution than a uniform improvement: 2B SFT and 2B+DPO have nearly identical partial-credit rates (35.4 in both rows of Table 

5

). We therefore frame DPO as a refinement on top of SFT, not a substitute, with real on-policy preferences the natural next step; Appendix 

K

 walks through five paired case studies: the agent-contract transfer (

K.1

), three DPO improvement mechanisms (

K.2

–

K.4

), and a failure that survives DPO (

K.5

).

Tool space and backbone capacity.

The 3-tool ablation crystallizes a capacity-dependent trade-off: the full tool set adds cognitive overhead, while the compact tool set removes specialized operations needed by some questions; larger backbones appear more robust to the former but still lose coverage when OCR or perspective correction is actually required—so tool-surface design should be treated as a model-capacity choice rather than a fixed property of the environment.

What the VDR ablation says about agent design.

That three extra steps on VDR convert 
no_answer
 into 
wrong_entity
 rather than 
correct
 suggests that the limiting factor at small scales is not exploration but verification: the agent identifies the relevant region and retrieves plausible passages, but lacks a reliable habit of cross-checking the retrieved evidence against the question. A second-pass verification step, or a process reward model in the style of 
[
17
, 
18
]
, is a natural direction.

Reproducibility.

All ten model cells train with parameter-efficient LoRA on one eight-GPU node and are evaluated under the same agent environment and GPT-5 judge; we release training configurations, agent code, data pipelines, the judge prompt, and the search-cache schema (Appendix 

J

).

Conclusion.

We presented LiteSearch-VL, a low-compute recipe for adapting Qwen3-VL-2B/4B into multimodal search agents from released teacher trajectories. Across four short-answer benchmarks and 12,400 GPT-5-judged rollouts, the main transfer is the agent contract—full-trajectory SFT changes the 2B model from almost never answering to 28.4% macro Pass@1—while compact tool distillation exposes a capacity-dependent trade-off and synthetic step-level DPO adds a smaller refinement (best 4B configuration: 30.8% macro Pass@1 and 37.1% partial-credit). The VDR ablation shows that extra search turns mainly convert abstentions into wrong-entity answers, pointing to verification-aware training as the next step.

Appendix C 
Judge Confidence Saturation

We verified directly against the raw judge logs that 
every
 judgment—all 12,400 main-evaluation rows, and all 14,400 rows once the four VDR step-budget ablation cells (Section 

4

) are included—reports 
confidence
=2; the field never takes the value 0 or 1 anywhere in our data. We do not read this as evidence that every judgment is correct. Two properties of our setup make it plausible: (i) all four benchmarks use short factoid references (a name, date, or number), so deciding whether the model’s response matches the reference is usually a clear-cut string/entity comparison rather than a graded call, which is precisely the case our rubric (Appendix 

G

) defines as 
confidence
=2; and (ii) LLM self-rated confidence scores are known to saturate toward the top of a scale regardless of the rubric’s intended granularity. Because 
confidence
 is not used to filter, reweight, or otherwise affect any reported Pass@1, partial-credit, or error-mode number, this saturation does not bias our headline results, but it does mean the field carries no discriminative signal in our evaluation and should be read as a pipeline-stability diagnostic (JSON parses, a value is always emitted) rather than an independent reliability check.

Appendix J 
Reproducibility Checklist

•
 

Code and configs.
 All ten model cells in
Table 

1

 are produced by configuration files that
share a common template; the only differences are the base model,
the dataset, and the training stage.

•
 

Data pipeline.
 The preprocessing script rebuilds the
train / validation split and the step-level expansion deterministically
given a fixed seed.

•
 

Tool environment.
 The agent loop, tool dispatcher, and
search cache are shared across the interactive playground and the
evaluation harness, ensuring identical inference behavior.

•
 

Judge.
 The GPT-5 judge prompt is fixed
(Appendix 

G

) and applied identically to every
model; the judge’s self-rated 
confidence
 field is 2 (the
maximum) on every individual judged row, not merely on average per
cell. We do not treat this as a reliability guarantee — see the
discussion in Section 

3

 for why we believe this reflects
the clear-cut nature of factoid matching in our benchmarks rather than
a well-calibrated confidence signal.

•
 

Caching.
 Search-API results are cached on disk and shared
across all ten model cells in a single evaluation pass; error-shaped
observations are not cached.

Appendix L 
Broader Impacts

Making tool-using multimodal search agents workable at 2B–4B parameters lowers the compute and cost barrier to deploying them, which is broadly useful for on-device and low-resource settings and is the motivation for this work. The same accessibility carries a risk that our own error analysis makes concrete: the resulting agents retrieve real web evidence yet still commit confidently to a wrong primary entity on a substantial fraction of questions (Section 4, Appendix K), and the VDR ablation shows that giving them more search turns converts abstentions into confident errors rather than correct answers. Deployments should therefore not treat these agents’ answers as verified, and we identify verification-aware training as the priority for future work. We release no new model weights or scraped corpora with this submission.
